What reconstitution actually means

Peptides are supplied as a dry (lyophilised) powder. Reconstitution is the process of adding liquid to that powder so it can be drawn into a syringe and injected.

This step determines every future dose. If you get this wrong, every injection after it is wrong too.

What you need to reconstitute peptides

  • Lyophilised peptide vial (e.g. 5 mg, 10 mg)
  • Bacteriostatic water (not sterile saline)
  • Alcohol swabs
  • Insulin syringe (for injection)
  • Larger syringe + needle (optional, for drawing water)
Non-negotiable

Always use bacteriostatic water unless explicitly instructed otherwise.

Why bacteriostatic water matters

Bacteriostatic water contains benzyl alcohol, which inhibits bacterial growth once the vial is punctured.

  • Allows multi-dose use
  • Extends usable lifespan after mixing
  • Reduces contamination risk

Using sterile water instead shortens shelf life and increases contamination risk.

Understanding dilution (this is where people fail)

Reconstitution does not change how much peptide you have — it changes how concentrated it is.

Example:

  • Vial contains: 10 mg peptide
  • You add: 2 mL bacteriostatic water
  • Result: 5 mg per mL

Every dose you draw must be calculated from that ratio.

Reality check

More water does not give you more peptide. It just spreads it thinner.

Step-by-step reconstitution process

  1. Clean vial tops with alcohol
  2. Draw bacteriostatic water into syringe
  3. Slowly inject water down the vial wall
  4. Do not blast directly onto the powder
  5. Gently swirl — never shake
  6. Allow powder to fully dissolve

Common reconstitution mistakes

  • Guessing dilution instead of calculating
  • Shaking the vial aggressively
  • Using the wrong water
  • Forgetting vial strength
  • Changing dilution mid-vial
Pattern

Most “peptides didn’t work” stories are math errors.
