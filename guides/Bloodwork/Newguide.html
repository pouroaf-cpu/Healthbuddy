Bloodwork basics for TRT

For people on TRT who want a simple, practical breakdown of which blood tests matter, when to get them, and how to stop flying blind.

Category: Bloodwork Level: Beginner Reading time: ~7 minutes Last updated: 08 Dec 2025

Why this matters

TRT isn’t just about chasing a “good” total testosterone number. Your bloodwork gives you a snapshot of how your whole system is responding – hormones, blood thickness, lipids, organs, and more. If you only look at one number, you miss half the story.

Good labs make conversations with your prescriber less emotional and more objective. Instead of “I feel off, can we just increase my dose?”, you can point to concrete changes in hematocrit, estradiol, or lipids and talk through options with data.

Most people don’t need a 40-marker “TRT panel” every time. You need a core set of tests done at sensible intervals, plus a few extras if you’ve got specific risks or symptoms that need digging into.

Big picture

Bloodwork turns TRT from guesswork into a feedback loop – dose, test, adjust, repeat.

What you should actually test

Below is a simple “core panel” that covers most people on TRT. Your prescriber may add or remove tests based on your history, medication, and risk factors.

Core panel

  • Total testosterone (TT) – the main signal of where your overall testosterone level is landing on your current dose and schedule.
  • Free testosterone (FT) – the portion of testosterone that’s not bound and is available for tissues to use. Often tracks better with symptoms than TT alone.
  • Sex hormone–binding globulin (SHBG) – helps explain why two people on the same dose can feel very different. High or low SHBG changes how much FT you get from a given TT.
  • Estradiol (E2, usually sensitive assay in men) – tracks how much testosterone is being converted to estrogen. Both very low and very high can cause issues.
  • Complete blood count (CBC) – especially hematocrit (HCT) and hemoglobin (HGB) to watch for blood thickening on TRT.
  • Comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP) – looks at kidney function, liver enzymes, and electrolytes. Useful baseline safety check while on any long-term medication.
  • Lipid panel – cholesterol and triglycerides. TRT, lifestyle, and bodyweight can all move these numbers over time.

Optional or situational tests

  • Prolactin – useful if you have low libido, erectile issues, or nipple sensitivity that doesn’t line up with your testosterone and estradiol picture.
  • PSA (prostate-specific antigen) – often checked in older men or those with prostate history as part of general safety monitoring.
  • TSH and basic thyroid panel – worth checking if you have fatigue, weight gain, or cold intolerance that doesn’t match your testosterone levels.
  • Fasting glucose or HbA1c – more relevant if you’re overweight, insulin resistant, or have a family history of diabetes.

When to test

You don’t need labs every few weeks. You need them at a few key decision points, then on a steady rhythm once things are stable.

  • Before starting TRT: Get a full baseline panel before your first injection or prescription. You want TT, FT, SHBG, E2, CBC, CMP, lipids, and whatever else your prescriber recommends so you know where you started.
  • After a change in dose, ester, or frequency: Re-check labs roughly 6–8 weeks after a meaningful change. That gives enough time for levels and symptoms to settle into the new pattern.
  • Long term, once stable: Many people do a full panel every 6–12 months, with CBC and hematocrit checked a bit more frequently if they tend to run high. Some prescribers prefer 3–6-monthly labs in the first year.

Common questions

Do I need the exact same panel every time?

Not always. Most people get a more complete panel at baseline and after big changes, then a slimmer “maintenance” set of labs once things are stable. The core markers above are a good starting point to discuss with your prescriber.

What if I feel bad but my numbers look “fine”?

Labs don’t replace symptoms. They give context. If you feel off, your prescriber may look at timing of injections, SHBG, estradiol, sleep, stress, and other health issues – not just a single TT result.

Can I adjust my dose just based on labs?

No. Labs are one input into dose decisions, alongside symptoms, blood pressure, medical history, and the risks your prescriber is balancing. Treat them as a decision tool, not a DIY green light for pushing doses up or down on your own.

Key takeaways

  • Use a consistent core panel instead of random one-off tests.
  • Time your labs around key decisions – baseline, after changes, then on a steady rhythm.
